Mission

Capability, not dependency.

The same model that can write your essay can also teach you to write a better one. Nothing in the technology settles which of those you get; the product does. That choice — made quietly, a hundred times over, in the design of a tool — is the entire difference between software that grows a person and software that hollows one out.

A tool that hands over the answer leaves you exactly where it found you, only faster. It produces output, and output is worth something. But understanding produces judgement, and judgement is what you bring to the problem the tool has never seen. One of those compounds across a career. The other has to be bought again every single time you need it.

We are not interested in whether people can be replaced. We are interested in how far a person can be taken by a machine that is genuinely on their side. That is a design problem before it is a modelling problem, and almost nobody is treating it as one.

Every product we ship has to pass one test: does the person close the tab more capable than they opened it? Not better served — more capable. If the honest answer is no, we built the wrong thing, however well it demos.

What we’re building and why

The bottleneck was never access to information.

Every explanation of every subject has been a search away for twenty years, and it did not make anyone an expert. Lectures went free. Textbooks went online. Encyclopaedias became a reflex. The material was never the scarce part, which is why adding more of it never worked.

What is scarce is someone who knows what you specifically do not know, in what order your gaps have to be repaired, and whether the repair was still holding a month later. That is measurement, sequencing, and scheduling — three jobs software ought to be extraordinarily good at, and which a human tutor can only do for one student at a time, at a price almost nobody pays. The best-evidenced intervention in education has always been the one that does not scale.

What changed is that models can now read a body of material and reason about its structure, rather than merely retrieve strings from it. A model can pull the concepts out of a lecture PDF, work out which of them depend on which, notice that a wrong answer implicates a prerequisite three steps further back, and write the single explanation that unblocks the student in front of it. The expensive half of tutoring — building and maintaining a model of one learner against one body of material — is finally something you can build.

Tutoring is the first place we are pointing that at, but the shape of the problem is not unique to studying. Most fields where people get better slowly have the same three parts: no honest measurement of where someone actually is, no ordering that starts from there, and nothing that checks whether the gain survived the month. Wherever those three go missing, the expensive expert is doing them by hand for one person at a time — and that is the job we think this technology should take, in one domain after another.

That capability points in two directions. Aimed one way, you get a machine that produces the assignment. Aimed the other, you get a machine that produces the student. We only build the second, in whatever domain we are working in, and it constrains every product we ship in three ways we have chosen to accept.

  • Grounded, or it doesn’t ship.

    Every lesson our software writes cites the passage of your own material it was drawn from, and the citation opens. A tutor that is fluently wrong is worse than no tutor at all — and fluency is precisely what these models are best at. Retrieval against your sources, rather than recall from training, is not a feature we added. It is the reason the thing can be trusted.

  • Measured before taught.

    We examine first. A curriculum built on an assumption about what you already know wastes your time at one end and loses you at the other. Teaching should begin at the exact point your understanding stops, and finding that point is real work — not a preference slider labelled beginner, intermediate, advanced.

  • Retained, or it didn’t count.

    A concept you understood in April and cannot use in June was never learned; it was rented. Scheduling review before the forgetting curve takes it back is unglamorous, invisible in a demo, and most of the actual result. We would rather be judged on what a person still knows next term.

Products · 01 — Cognometrist

First out: an AI tutor for the material you actually have to learn.

You bring the syllabus: notes, slides, textbook chapters, papers, whatever the course really runs on. Cognometrist reads all of it, maps every concept it contains, examines where your understanding stops, and teaches forward from exactly there — citing your own pages, line by line. Then it keeps what you learned from quietly decaying.

  1. Embeddings

    Your material becomes the syllabus. Embedding models read every passage you uploaded, so page forty counts as much as page one.

  2. Knowledge graph

    Concepts and their prerequisites are extracted and mapped, so the order of teaching is derived from the subject rather than guessed at by a curriculum designer who never met you.

  3. Examiner

    A source-grounded exam locates the edge of what you already know — including the gaps you would not have thought to report.

  4. Content creator

    One lesson, written for the one concept you are ready for next, with every claim marked against the passages it came from.

  5. Spaced review

    Every mastered concept resurfaces just before you would have forgotten it — daily, then weekly, then monthly — so understanding compounds instead of fading.

It will not teach a concept whose prerequisites are unproven, so a gap has nowhere to hide. If you later slip on something you had mastered, it takes the mastery back and teaches the concept again. The goal was never a good study session. The goal is that in June you still know it.

What comes next

One product is a proof, not a company.

Cognometrist exists to show the thesis holds somewhere it can be checked. Studying is a good first domain precisely because it is unforgiving: either the learner can answer the question in June or they cannot, and no amount of pleasant interface covers the difference. A method that survives that is worth pointing at something else.

So the roadmap is not a list of features for one product. It is the same three-part machine — measure honestly, sequence from where the person actually is, verify the gain held — rebuilt for the next domain where expertise is currently rationed by the cost of an expert's attention. We are deliberately not naming those here. Announcing a product before it works is the sort of claim this page has spent several sections arguing against, and we would rather be judged on what has shipped.

If you are working on something in that shape, or you want to know what we are building next before it is public, the fastest route is to write to us directly.

How we work

Principles we’d like to be held to.

These are not aspirations posted on a wall. Each one is a rule that has already cost us something — a feature we did not ship, a claim we could not make, a shortcut we did not take.

  • Name the machinery.

    We tell people what the software actually does — embeddings, a knowledge graph, an examiner, retrieval-grounded generation — instead of selling “AI” as a mood. Users make better decisions when the mechanism is legible, and we write better software when we have to describe it plainly.

  • Ship the whole loop.

    A prompt wrapped in a text box is not a product. The valuable work is everything in between: ingestion, structure, evaluation, scheduling, and the state that has to survive a user closing the laptop for a week and coming back.

  • Earn the claim.

    If we say a lesson is grounded in your sources, there is a citation you can open and check. Every sentence of marketing should map to a mechanism a sceptical engineer could go and verify. Where we cannot show the mechanism, we do not make the claim.

  • The person stays the author.

    Our tools do not hand in your work for you. This is not a compliance posture; it is the product thesis. Software that does the work for you removes, one convenience at a time, the reason to be able to do the work at all.

  • Design around the failure mode.

    Language models are confidently wrong sometimes — a property of the technology, not a bug awaiting a patch. So we build for it: retrieval instead of recall, citations instead of assurances, and examinations that check the learner rather than take anyone’s word for it.

  • Prefer the durable result.

    We optimise for what someone retains in a month, not for how productive they felt in a session. Those two numbers disagree far more often than the industry likes to admit, and when they do, we take the first one.
